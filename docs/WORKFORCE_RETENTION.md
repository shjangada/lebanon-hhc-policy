# Workforce & medicine retention in the Beqaa: evidence and design implications (as of 21 Nov 2026)

**Question.** What makes doctors, nurses, other health workers, and medicines/equipment *stay* in Lebanon, and especially in rural Beqaa / Baalbek-Hermel? Which of these levers survive our constraints (low funds, instability, weak infrastructure, few professionals, "no new doctors", state-capacity and community-decision goals, deliverable by 2030)?

**Rules used here.** Every figure has a source ID (W01...) listed in section 10 and in `data/registry/workforce_sources.csv`. **[secondary]** means the figure comes from press or a third party quoting the original. **[assumption]** means it is our design choice, not evidence. Where no Beqaa-specific number exists, this says so. No figure was invented.

---

## 0. Bottom line (five findings)

1. **The exodus is large, and it hollowed out public and rural care first.** WHO estimated in 2021 that ~40% of doctors and ~30% of nurses had left since Oct 2019 (W01 [secondary]); MoPH told WHO in 2024 that ~2,000 physicians and ~3,000 nurses had migrated (W02); K2P (Oct 2025) still uses 40%/30% and adds that **~70% of public hospitals are understaffed** (W05). Lebanon's largest public hospital ran ~200 of 430 beds in 2024, partly for lack of money for staff (W29).
2. **Pay in usable currency is the dominant driver, and dollarization is the one lever with return evidence. But it reached the private sector, not public or rural staff.** At Rafik Hariri University Hospital (RHUH), 70% of surveyed staff intended to migrate. 93% of them cited finances and 81% security, and 58% earned only $65–129 a month (W07, Feb 2026). The Order of Physicians says about half of the ~3,500 doctors who left had returned by mid-2024, after it got insurers to pay doctors' fees in dollars. Yet only 11% of RHUH's leavers returned (W03 [secondary]). In 2023 only ~20% of hospitals (mostly university hospitals) paid nurses any fresh dollars, and public-sector nurses got none (W06).
3. **War is now a co-equal push factor in the Beqaa.** 226 health workers and patients were killed in Lebanon between Oct 2023 and Nov 2024; 47% of attacks on health were fatal, the highest share of any active conflict (W12). The cumulative count of health workers killed reached 257 by 10 Mar 2026 (W13), and WHO counted 128 more killed in the three months after 2 March 2026 (W15 [secondary]). Hostilities explicitly covered Bekaa and Baalbek (W13, W14).
4. **What can realistically keep services running by 2030 is not new doctors. It is (a) stabilizing pay for the staff already there, (b) widening what nurses, midwives and CHWs may do, (c) remote specialist support, and (d) a reliable MoPH medicine pipeline.** Lebanon already runs all four at pilot or national scale: AaSalameh nurse–midwife–CHW teams (W23), NCD task-sharing in rural clinics (W24), the MoPH–WHO–EU chronic medication programme reaching Baalbek-Hermel PHCCs (W20, W21), and the 2025–2030 digital health strategy plus diaspora tele-teaching (W26, W27). The WHO 2021 rural guideline recommends exactly this kind of *bundled* package (W16).
5. **Donor-paid top-ups work in the short run but build a parallel state.** Donors fund ~40% of the health sector (Minister Abiad, W17). The basic PHC package (LPSP) is entirely donor-funded and ~67% of PHCCs are NGO-run (W05). NGOs out-pay the state and drain its staff (W17). Meanwhile the state pays an official public minimum wage worth ~$7.50 (W19) and in Feb 2026 approved a six-salary allowance financed by VAT that is still being phased in (W18). **Design implication:** any top-up must be pegged to the public pay scale, flow through PHCCs or Unions of Municipalities under community oversight, and taper into the MoPH or public allowance on a published schedule (section 7).

## 4. Medicines and equipment: what keeps supply in rural areas

- **MoPH chronic medication programme (with WHO, EU-funded).** MoPH publishes delivery lists by centre. In Sep–Oct 2024 these included Zahle and West Bekaa PHCCs and Baalbek-Hermel centres such as Chlifa and Chaat (W20). By Jan 2025 MoPH reported more than three million medicine boxes delivered to PHCCs serving the displaced, through MoPH, WHO, UNICEF and bilateral donors (W21). The historical YMCA-run chronic-drug channel is documented only by a 2014 essential list (UNHCR portal). **We did not find a current 2025–26 YMCA programme document. Verify before naming YMCA in the memo.**
- **EIB–WHO €10M grant (signed 6 Mar 2025):** rehabilitates the Central Public Health Laboratory and supplies medication to PHCCs (W22).
- **Cost structure:** medicines are ~29% and lab ~34% of LPSP facility costs, vs staff ~22% (W05). Staff are cheap relative to medicines, so retaining staff is a *low-cost* lever compared with stockpiling.
- **Continuity under war:** in the 2024 escalation, 241 of 317 PHCCs kept operating, and 120 PHC satellite units (half UNICEF-supported) served ~231,000 displaced people in >1,000 shelters (W28). In 2026 MSF supplied chronic medicines and set up a paediatric ICU at Baalbek Governmental Hospital, with 6,760 NCD consultations in Bekaa since 2 March (W30).
- **Equipment maintenance** is named as a critical gap alongside staff shortages (WHO/Amel visit, Sept 2025; not separately registered).

## 7. Top-ups: the dependence risk, and how an escrow/transition shifts it

**Risk (evidence).** Donor-paid salaries build "a parallel state, a parallel system" (Abiad, W17). Pay gaps between NGO and public posts pull staff out of public facilities (W17), and if two or three donors leave, services collapse (W17; W05 calls it donor fatigue). Paying top-ups through INGOs repeats this.

**Design (our proposal; parameters are [assumption] until sourced).**
1. **Peg, don't outbid.** The top-up is capped at a fixed share of the gap between current public pay and a reference wage (e.g. the private minimum wage, W19). It is never above what the public scale plus the 2026 allowance (W18) could plausibly reach by 2030. Then nobody is pulled from public posts into "project" posts.
2. **Pay the post, not the person.** Top-ups attach to MoPH-network PHCC and governmental-hospital posts in priority Beqaa towns. They cover nurses, midwives, CHWs and existing GPs on rotation, and are conditional on attendance verified by the community health committee.
3. **Route through the ring-fenced Beqaa window** in an existing pooled fund (FEASIBILITY: LHF/LFF), paid via the PHCC's own NGO or municipal operator. A Union of Municipalities is co-signatory and an NGO is fiduciary.
4. **Glide path into the state.** Each year, the donor share of the top-up falls and the MoPH/public-allowance share rises, on a schedule published in advance. Illustrative [assumption]: 100/0 in 2027, 75/25 in 2028, 50/50 in 2029, 25/75 in 2030. The capacity tranche releases the next step only if process triggers are met: posts filled ≥ threshold, transparent payroll, and MoPH has the post on its establishment list. **If MoPH cannot co-pay, the schedule pauses rather than collapsing.** The need floor keeps the minimum staffing paid (this matches the lock-out warning already in `escrow.py`).
5. **Exit test.** By 2030, success means top-up posts appear on MoPH/PHC Department payroll records or in the public allowance. It does not mean "donor money spent".

**Collateral-damage checks.** (a) *Internal brain drain*: top-ups in priority towns pull staff from neighbouring non-priority PHCCs, so monitor neighbour vacancies. (b) *Gaming*: attendance can be faked, so use community verification plus spot-checks. (c) *Equity*: CHW recruitment must include displaced Syrian women where communities are mixed, as AUB's MUH did (W25), while respecting legal limits on work. (d) *Fiscal*: a top-up the state can never absorb just postpones collapse. That is why the peg is tied to the public allowance trajectory. (e) *Security*: do not publish staff names or locations.

## 8. How this plugs into the models

**M1 access (E2SFCA supply side).** Replace static `capacity_weekly` with
`S_j = Σ_cadre FTE_cadre,j × consults_per_FTE_cadre × fill_rate_j × open_j`.
- `fill_rate_j` comes from scenario: *baseline attrition*, *war shock* (2026 pattern: PHCC closures, W14), *top-up retained*, *task-shifted*.
- Task-shifting raises effective capacity without new doctors: nurse/midwife NCD and ANC follow-ups count as consults (W23, W24).
- Tele-consults add specialist capacity at the hub but **not** physical access. Model them as raising the referral-completion probability, not `S_j`.
- Closure scenario: reuse the WHO closed-facility counts (sitreps W13/W14) and set `open_j = 0` with `fill_rate` carried to a neighbouring facility (staff displacement).

**M3 referral/staffing simulation (not yet built).**
- Monthly state per facility: posts, filled, attrition hazard `h = h0 × (pay_gap)^β × security_multiplier`. **h0, β and the multiplier are [assumption].** Calibrate their direction from W07 (finance 93%, security 81%) and their magnitude from any registry data obtained.
- Levers as inputs: `topup_share` (0–1), `task_shift_share`, `rotation_days_per_month`, `telemed_on`.
- Outputs: ANC4 and NCD follow-up completion, referral completion, and months with a PHCC below minimum staffing. These feed the Workforce page `staff_fill_rate` slider, which is already specified in CLAUDE.md.

**M5 cost model.** Add cost lines (all **to be sourced, mock until then**):
- `topup_per_FTE_month` by cadre. Anchor the plausible range on W07 salary bands ($65–129) and the private minimum wage (~$285, W19). Do not invent a number; enter it as a parameter with a source column.
- `chw_stipend_month`, `chw_training_once` (AaSalameh/MUH costs to request from UNICEF/AUB).
- `rotation_transport_day`, `telemed_kit_once`, `telemed_connectivity_month`.
- `medicine_pack_patient_year`. Note: medicines (~29%) outweigh staff (~22%) in LPSP costs (W05).
- Show the **donor vs MoPH share per year** from the section 7 glide path. This is the dependency metric.

**Escrow (`escrow.py`).** `posts_filled_min` already exists. Add `topup_state_share_min` per year as a trigger, and report the "share of retention spending on the public payroll" as the state-capacity outcome.
